Most guides to commercial snow removal in Toronto open with prices. That is the wrong starting point. For a plaza, condo, office or industrial site, the contract is mainly a risk-transfer document, and the cheapest bid is often the one that leaves the most risk with you. This guide starts with the rules and the law, then moves to contract structure, cost, salt and how to vet a contractor, so you can compare bids on what actually protects the property.

What Toronto Requires of Property Owners

Three City rules shape a winter plan. The details can change, so confirm the current wording on toronto.ca before you finalize a contract.

  • Sidewalks next to your property (Chapter 719). The Municipal Code makes owners and occupants responsible for clearing the adjoining public sidewalk within 12 hours of the end of a snowfall, but a 1999 amendment exempts them where the City clears the sidewalk under its policy. Today the City starts sidewalk clearing at 2 cm of accumulation. The City's guidance says that under 2 cm, residents and businesses clear adjacent sidewalks within 12 hours, and at 2 cm or more City crews do it.
  • Your own property (Property Standards, Chapter 629). City staff have described the requirement as clearing snow and ice from steps, landings, walks, driveways, parking spaces and ramps on private property within 24 hours.
  • Where you put the snow. Shoveling, pushing or plowing snow from private property onto City roads, cycling lanes, sidewalks or laneways is prohibited. A November 2025 City report puts the fine at $610, including a $110 victim surcharge, and notes enforcement is mostly complaint-driven.

That last rule matters when you compare bids. A contractor whose plan depends on pushing piles to the curb is creating a fine for you. Ask where snow will be stored on site, and whether hauling is included or priced per load.

Policy watch: in 2025 City Council asked staff about making corporate property owners responsible for abutting sidewalks again. The staff report (November 2025) described significant practical challenges and recommended the committee receive it for information. Rules can still change, so check before each season.

Who Is Liable When Someone Slips? Ontario's Occupiers' Liability Act

In Ontario, slip-and-fall claims on commercial property are governed by the Occupiers' Liability Act. The owner, the property manager, a tenant in control of the space and sometimes the contractor can all be "occupiers." Courts focus on who had control and responsibility for the hazard.

Four points every property manager should know

  • Hiring a contractor does not automatically end your exposure. Section 6(1) can protect an owner who hired an independent contractor, but legal commentary says the owner must show it acted reasonably in hiring a competent contractor and in checking that the work was done.
  • The 60-day notice rule. Since Bill 118 (2020) amended the Act, a person injured by snow or ice must give written notice to the occupier within 60 days. This makes quick incident documentation essential on your side too.
  • Contractors are judged mainly against the contract. A Landscape Ontario analysis notes that, absent special circumstances, a contractor is generally liable only if it fails to perform as the contract requires. If the contract is vague about triggers and response times, nobody can prove what was promised.
  • Indemnity clauses have limits. Ontario counsel note that an indemnity generally will not cover the owner's own negligence unless the wording clearly says so.

Real cases show how this plays out. In one Ontario condo case reported by Condo Adviser, the contractor was found to be the occupier after salting was delayed by 3.5 hours following its arrival. The lesson is not that contractors always carry the risk. It is that the contract, the visit records and the actual timing decide who does.

This is general information, not legal advice. Have a lawyer or your insurer review your contract and indemnity wording.

How Commercial Snow Contracts Are Structured

Most agreements are one of four shapes, or a hybrid. The choice decides who carries the weather risk.

HourlyTime and equipment billed by the hourLarge or irregular sites with loadersUnclear minimums and rates

Whichever you choose, the contract should state these in writing: the trigger depth (and whether it is measured in cm or inches), the response time after the trigger, the exact scope (lot, sidewalks, entrances, ramps, loading docks, fire routes), whether salt and de-icing are included or billed per application, and how snow storage and hauling are handled. Industry guides note that raising the trigger from 2 cm to 5 cm can lower monthly cost, but it also means more snow and ice sit on walkways, so weigh that against your risk.

Fees that surprise people

  • Surcharges for major storms or blizzards.
  • Hauling snow off site, which one Ontario contractor puts at $500 to $1,500 per load.
  • Emergency call-outs outside the trigger depth.
  • Salt billed per tonne rather than included.

Salt, Water and Why Contractors Are Being Asked About It

Road salt is effective and cheap, which is exactly why it gets overused. Chloride dissolves into storm sewers and flows to creeks and lakes. The Muskoka Watershed Council cites roughly 2 to 3 million tonnes of road salt applied annually in Ontario, and World Wildlife Fund Canada says about 70% of road salt contamination in the Great Lakes watershed comes from private property, such as large parking lots. Your lot is part of that story.

Ontario's answer is the Smart About Salt program, run by a not-for-profit council to improve salt practices on parking lots and sidewalks without compromising safety. Certification is voluntary, but Partners in Project Green's checklist for property managers asks whether your contractor holds it, and recommends considering low-chloride options such as brines. Researchers at Toronto Metropolitan University have noted that liquid anti-icing can cut the chloride reaching waterways, and may cost contractors more up front but save money later as salt prices rise.

What to do: ask for Smart About Salt certification, a written salt policy, calibrated application records and a plan that treats walkways before storms rather than over-salting afterward.

How to Vet a Commercial Snow Contractor

  • Insurance: request a certificate of insurance and confirm limits with your insurer. Ask your broker whether you should be named on the policy.
  • Documentation: require time-stamped visit logs, ideally with photos and salt notes, delivered to you on request or after every event.
  • Response commitments: a written time from trigger to arrival, and what happens when crews are stretched in a big storm.
  • Equipment and backup: ask what happens if a truck fails, and whether any work is subcontracted.
  • Salt competence: Smart About Salt certification or equivalent training.
  • Snow storage plan: where it goes and who pays to haul it.
  • References from comparable sites: a plaza with a heavy evening footfall is not a warehouse.

Your Own Records and Incident Response

Because liability can follow control and reasonableness, your own inspection habits matter. Walk the site on a schedule during winter events, keep dated notes and photos, and file the contractor's logs. If someone falls, record the date, time, location and conditions immediately, preserve the logs, and notify your insurer. With a 60-day written notice window for snow and ice claims, you will want those records within reach, not reconstructed months later.

A Pre-Season Timeline

  1. Early autumn: map every area you are responsible for, including sidewalks, ramps, entrances and loading zones.
  2. Before the first storm: collect two or three bids against the same written scope, and have counsel or your insurer review the contract and indemnity.
  3. Pre-season: sign the contract, confirm insurance certificates, and agree on contacts for storm events.
  4. First storms: check actual response times against the contract and ask for the logs.
  5. Mid-winter: review salt use and any complaints, and adjust the plan.
  6. Spring: reconcile invoices against logs and decide whether to renew.

A Note on Residential Properties

Everything above applies to commercial sites. Home driveways are a different market, usually priced per visit or per season. If you also manage a house or rental, platforms such as Ezi Home Services, which lists snow removal among its home services in Canadian cities including Toronto and operates in Dallas as well, are aimed at homeowners rather than commercial lots, so confirm scope and insurance before using any residential provider on a business property.

The Bottom Line

The best commercial snow removal contract in Toronto is not the cheapest. It is the one that spells out the trigger, response time, scope, salt policy and records, fits the City's rules, and leaves you able to show you acted reasonably. Budget on the order of $200 to $800 per lot visit or $1,000 to $2,500 a month for a mid-sized property, ask for Smart About Salt certification, and keep your own records. Start early, before the first storm sets the terms.
